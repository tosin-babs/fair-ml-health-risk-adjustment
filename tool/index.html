The Payment Formula

Health plans in Medicare Advantage and the ACA marketplaces are paid by a formula that predicts how much each enrollee will cost. When a formula guesses low for a group of people, plans lose money on them. This page tests formulas of that kind, from a linear model built the way payment formulas are to machine-learning models, on how they guess, who they shortchange, what it costs to fix, and how much more intensive diagnosis coding moves the money.

These are research benchmarks, not the actual CMS-HCC or HHS-HCC formulas. Every model here was estimated on the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey, a household survey with self-reported conditions, and predicts next year's spending from this year's record. The comparisons between models are the point; the dollar amounts are not what any real plan is paid.

01How well each formula predicts next year's spending

Each model saw one year of a person's record and predicted the next year's medical spending for people it had never seen. R² is the share of the variation in spending the model explains, for a single fitted model, with a 95% interval; a flexible model explains more, but none explains most of it, because much of next year's spending is genuinely unpredictable. The payment-form linear model keeps every diagnosis coefficient at zero or above, as payment formulas do; the unconstrained linear model pays some people a negative amount.

02Who gets shortchanged

Net compensation is what the formula pays for an average member of a group minus what that group actually costs, in dollars per person per year. Below zero, the plan loses money on the group and has a reason to avoid enrolling them.

03The price of closing the gap

A fairness penalty pushes the formula to pay each target group what it costs, at some loss of overall accuracy. Move the slider to strengthen the penalty and watch both numbers.
